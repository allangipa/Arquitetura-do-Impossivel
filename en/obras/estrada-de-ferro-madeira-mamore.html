Structure 07 · Brazil

Madeira-Mamoré Railroad

From Porto Velho to Guajará-Mirim, Rondônia, Brazil · 1907–1912

Laying track across more than 360 km of forest, swamp and rapids in the Amazon, where two companies had already given up and where almost every worker came down with malaria.

Between Porto Velho and Guajará-Mirim, in Rondônia, Brazil, the Madeira and Mamoré rivers run down a chain of waterfalls and rapids that no loaded boat could get past. To ship out the rubber of Bolivia and the upper Madeira, that stretch had to be bypassed overland: a railroad of 364 to 366 km cut through the forest, over swamps that the floods covered, in a place where malaria struck almost everyone.

Two companies tried in the nineteenth century and gave up. On the third attempt, Percival Farquhar's Madeira-Mamoré Railway Co. finished the line in 1912, after hiring 21,817 men. How many of them died is the question nobody settles: the records of the time stay under two thousand, and the most cited estimates speak of about six thousand.

01A railroad in the Acre treaty

On November 17, 1903, in Petrópolis, in the state of Rio de Janeiro, Brazil and Bolivia signed the treaty that gave Acre to Brazil. Under Article III, Brazil would pay £2,000,000 to Bolivia, in two installments, "since there is no equivalence in the areas." Under Article VII, it took on another obligation: to build a railroad from the port of Santo Antônio, on the Madeira River, to Guajará-Mirim, on the Mamoré, for both countries to use at the same rates.

The reason was the river. Between Santo Antônio and Guajará-Mirim, the Madeira and the Mamoré come down a staircase of waterfalls and rapids that no loaded boat could pass. The rubber of Bolivia and the upper Madeira needed an overland detour. The treaty gave four years, but chose its words carefully: Brazil "will endeavor" to finish within that time.

The railroad ended up starting from Porto Velho, some 7 km downriver from Santo Antônio, and it never entered Bolivia: it ends at Guajará-Mirim, on the Brazilian side of the Mamoré, across from Guayaramerín. And it was not the first time someone had tried to build it.

02Not with all the capital in the world

In 1872, the American colonel George Earl Church raised in London a Bolivian loan of £1,700,000 nominal, sold at 68%, and hired the British firm Public Works Construction Co. for £600,000. The engineers reached Santo Antônio on July 6, 1872. A year later, according to engineer Neville B. Craig, they had three and a half miles of survey, about 5.6 km, and not a foot of track laid.

On July 9, 1873, one year and three days after arriving, Public Works abandoned the contract and went to court in London. In its petition, quoted by Craig, the company described the region as a charnel house, with men dying like flies, and said that with all the capital in the world and half its population it would be impossible to build the railroad.

In 1878 came P. & T. Collins, of Philadelphia. The steamer Mercedita sailed on January 3 with 220 people. The second ship, the Metropolis, carrying rails and workers, ran aground and broke apart off Currituck Beach, North Carolina, on January 31, 1878, before ever leaving the United States. Craig counts 80 railroad employees dead; the North Carolina historical marker speaks of 85 dead in all; other counts reach 102.

Of the 941 people Collins sent from the United States, 719 reached Santo Antônio, and at least 23.6% of them died, according to Craig's table, which draws a comparison: Union soldiers in the Civil War died at a lower rate. Of the mortality among the roughly 400 men from Ceará and 200 Bolivian Indigenous workers hired in the region, again according to Craig, no record was kept. In 1879 the work stopped. Craig lists what was left: about 3.5 km of permanent track and 6.3 km over which the construction train ran; the 7 km of line usually cited depend on what is being measured. A Baldwin locomotive was also left behind at Santo Antônio.

03Track laid on branches

The concession was won by Joaquim Catramby, who signed a contract on November 14, 1906. The man who got it moving was the American Percival Farquhar, who bought the concession and founded the Madeira-Mamoré Railway Co. in 1907; the formal transfer came through Decree 6,838 of June 30, 1908. The contractor was May, Jekyll & Randolph. According to historian Abnael Machado de Lima, the first crew left Belém on June 9, 1907, with 140 people, and the symbolic start of work was on July 4, in Porto Velho.

The route was the problem. The photographs the American Dana B. Merrill took of the work, between 1909 and 1912, show what their captions describe: in the swampy stretch along the Abunã River, track laid on corduroy beds of brushwood and palm trunks so the ballast train could run over the water; temporary wooden bridges over the forest streams; rock blasted open with dynamite; embankments washed out by rain; derailed cars. In one of them, the caption notes that in flood season the Madeira rose 14 meters above its low-water level.

The first section, about 90 km to Jaci-Paraná, was handed over on May 31, 1910. In November of that year, a contract amendment began paying £2,750 per kilometer of ballast and temporary bridges. The Federal Audit Court refused to register it, and only Legislative Decree 2,579 of June 7, 1912, validated the expense, according to an opinion on the contracts published by Conjur.

04The demon of the Amazon

The greatest enemy was not in the route. From 1908 the company ran Candelária Hospital, between Santo Antônio and Porto Velho. In 1910 and 1911 it had eleven doctors, some with experience on the Panama Canal, and the service was headed by the American Carl Lovelace. According to the survey by historian André Vasques Vital, the hospital took in 120 to 150 patients a day in 1911, and 60 in 1912.

In 1910, the company itself hired Oswaldo Cruz. He traveled with Belisário Penna from June 16 to August 29 and observed the work from July 9 to August 7. According to historians Júlio César Schweickardt and Nísia Trindade Lima, Cruz called malaria the demon of the Amazon and recorded more than 90% of the workers as sick; another reading of the same report speaks of 80 to 90%. A worker stayed on the job, on average, less than three months.

The response was to make medicine a work rule. From August 1, 1910, quinine became a daily, compulsory dose, and using the anti-mosquito measures became "a work obligation." Merrill photographed men wearing mesh hoods over their heads. And malaria was not the only disease: a report by Lovelace, cited in an article about the region, counts 963 cases of beriberi at Candelária from 1908 to 1911.

05The count nobody settles

The company's table, reproduced by the writer Hiram Reis e Silva, adds up to 21,817 workers brought in from outside and 1,552 deaths between 1907 and 1912, with more than 400 deaths a year from 1909 to 1911. It is the most repeated figure. But there is a variant of the same table whose totals do not add up, a version that gives 1,522 and another, attributed to Manoel Rodrigues Ferreira and Francisco Foot Hardman, that gives 1,693. All of them stay under two thousand.

The record counts what the hospital saw. It does not count those who died along the way, on the trip to Manaus or Belém, or after going home sick. That is why the estimates climb: about 6,000 in the most cited count, which also appears attributed to the Brazilian government; 6,208 in the count by Hiram Reis e Silva, who multiplies the recorded deaths by four; 7,000 to 10,000 in novelist Errol Lincoln Uys; more than 30,000 in Fiorelo Picoli. At the other end, Ricardo Leite, in his 2020 book 1912 – Vitória na Selva, says that even adding 20% for unrecorded deaths the total stays under two thousand, and calls the counts of 6,000 to 20,000 speculation.

The archive that could break the tie no longer exists: the railroad's papers were burned when it was shut down. Of Merrill's plates, 189 negatives survive, now at the Museu Paulista of the University of São Paulo. One of them shows the Candelária cemetery, in a clearing behind the hospital. Craig had already written, in 1907, about the Collins men who died after going home: there was no way of knowing how many.

06Finished too late

The last crosstie was laid on April 30, 1912, and the inauguration appears under different dates depending on the source, between April and September of that year. The market that justified the project had already turned. Between 1910 and 1911, the top price of fine rubber fell by nearly half; the Amazon's share of world supply went from 44% to 35% between 1910 and 1912; and over that decade the plantations of Asia overtook Brazilian production, according to a study published in the journal Almanack.

The railroad stayed, and with it a city: Porto Velho was born of the railroad. Between 1910 and 1912, Chicago Bridge & Iron Works put up three water tanks there of 200,000 liters each, which supplied the city and the railroad until the 1950s and today appear on the municipal flag. The Baldwin locomotive that Collins left at Santo Antônio in 1879 was restored, named Coronel Church, and stands in the railroad museum in Porto Velho.

Decree 58,501 of May 25, 1966, shut down the line and handed it to the Ministry of War; the last train ran in 1972. The nickname that stuck, the Devil's Railroad, is the title of Manoel Rodrigues Ferreira's 1959 book. Before it, Ferreira published newspaper reports with photos printed from Merrill's negatives, which he received in 1956.

07The tracks today

In the 2000s, IPHAN listed part of what remained as heritage: a piece of the Porto Velho rail yard, the tracks to Santo Antônio, the three water tanks and the Candelária cemetery. The rest of the line was left out. On October 28, 2025, the Federal Public Prosecutor's Office sued Rondônia, the federal government and IPHAN, seeking permanent heritage protection for all 366 km and R$6 million in collective moral damages, over stolen rails, dismantled steel bridges, ripped-out crossties and abandoned locomotives.

In August 2026, according to News Rondônia, the Porto Velho city government reached an agreement with the Brazilian Railway Preservation Association to restore the structure and sent IPHAN a letter of intent about a bid for UNESCO World Heritage status, still at an early stage. In September, the city government announced for early October the return of the train ride along the Madeira-Mamoré tracks to the church of Santo Antônio; according to Mayor Léo Moraes, the rolling stock had sat idle for almost three decades.

MYTHSWhat people say and what the record shows

  • What people say

    One man died for every crosstie on the railroad.

    What the record shows

    It is a catchphrase, not a count. It appears in print in 1942 and spread in the following decades, even in versions of twenty dead per crosstie. The line has from 170,000 to about 549,000 crossties, depending on the calculation, far above any estimate of the dead.

  • What people say

    30,000 men died building it.

    What the record shows

    The number appears in one author, Fiorelo Picoli. The records of the time count fewer than two thousand dead, and the most cited estimates, about six thousand. "About 30,000" also circulates as the number of workers, not of dead, and the confusion probably starts there.

  • What people say

    The railroad was built by slaves.

    What the record shows

    None of the sources consulted says so. Slavery had ended in Brazil in 1888, and the company's table counts 21,817 men hired and brought in from outside; Merrill photographed workers receiving their pay beside a track car. What life was really like for those who worked there, far from everything and sick, is another question.

  • What people say

    The nickname Devil's Railroad dates from the construction years.

    What the record shows

    It was popularized by the book A Ferrovia do Diabo, by Manoel Rodrigues Ferreira, published in 1959. No earlier use has been found.

  • What people say

    The Madeira-Mamoré was shut down in 1966.

    What the record shows

    1966 is the year of the decree that deactivated it. The last train ran in 1972.

QUESTIONSFrequently asked questions

How many people died building the Madeira-Mamoré Railroad?

Nobody settles the count. The records of the time give 1,552, 1,522 or 1,693 deaths, all under two thousand; the most cited estimate speaks of about 6,000, and some authors go from 7,000 to 10,000 or to more than 30,000, because nobody recorded who died outside the hospital.

When was the Madeira-Mamoré Railroad built?

Between 1907 and 1912. The symbolic start was on July 4, 1907, in Porto Velho, and the last crosstie was laid on April 30, 1912; the inauguration date varies among sources, between April and September of that year.

Why was the Madeira-Mamoré Railroad built?

To bypass the waterfalls and rapids of the Madeira and Mamoré rivers, which no loaded boat could get past, and to ship out the rubber of Bolivia and the upper Madeira. Brazil took on the obligation in the Treaty of Petrópolis, signed with Bolivia in 1903.

Who built the Madeira-Mamoré Railroad?

The Madeira-Mamoré Railway Co., founded in 1907 by the American Percival Farquhar, with the contractor May, Jekyll & Randolph, which hired 21,817 workers brought in from outside. It was the third attempt: two companies had already given up, in 1873 and in 1879.

Does the Madeira-Mamoré Railroad still run?

The line was shut down by decree in 1966, and the last train ran in 1972. In September 2026, the Porto Velho city government announced the return of the train ride to Santo Antônio for early October.
