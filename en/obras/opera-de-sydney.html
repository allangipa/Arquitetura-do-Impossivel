Sydney Opera House

Bennelong Point, Sydney, New South Wales, Australia · 1959–1973

To turn into concrete a sketch of shells drawn freehand, which matched no known geometric form and which, for years, nobody managed to describe in numbers, calculate or fabricate.

In January 1957, a 38-year-old Danish architect, Jørn Utzon, won the competition for Sydney's opera house, in Australia, with drawings that were little more than a sketch: white, curved, free-flowing shells, opening over the harbor at Bennelong Point. The jury went with the drawing. Nobody yet knew how to build it.

Construction began in 1959 with the roof still unsolved. The engineers at Ove Arup & Partners spent years trying to turn those curves into parabolas and ellipses that could be calculated and fabricated. The answer, in 1961, was to cut all the shells from the surface of a single sphere. The rest of the story, with the architect's departure, a budget multiplied by 14 and a lottery that paid the bill, ran until 1973.

01A sketch without geometry

In 1956 the New South Wales government launched an international competition for an opera house at Bennelong Point, the spit of land in Sydney Harbour next to the Harbour Bridge. More than two hundred designs arrived. The winner, announced on January 29, 1957, was entry number 218, by the Dane Jørn Utzon: a stone podium and, on top of it, white shells opening toward the sea.

It was a drawing, not a design. The curves had been drawn freehand and matched no shape that could be written as an equation. Without an equation, there was no way to calculate the stresses at each point or to fabricate the pieces. Legend has it that Eero Saarinen, a member of the jury, pulled Utzon's design out of the pile of rejects; another juror, Henry Ashworth, denied the story, and there is no precise record of how the choice was made.

02Starting without a roof

Premier Joseph Cahill, according to accounts from the time, wanted to start quickly so that a later government could not cancel the project. Construction began on March 2, 1959, with the builder Civil & Civic doing the podium, while the roof still had no solution. Cahill died seven months later. The competition budget was £3.5 million, and completion was planned for 1963.

In London, the engineers at Ove Arup & Partners tried to give the shells a shape. First, sections of parabolas; then, of ellipsoids. Arup itself counts 12 schemes before reaching the solution. The initial idea of a thin concrete shell gave way to ribs, and every new geometry ran into the same problem: the shells had different curvatures at every point, and every piece would have to be unique. The podium, begun before the roof had a shape, was not finished until February 1963.

03Slices of a sphere

The way out, in 1961, was to abandon the free forms and cut all the shells from the surface of a single sphere. On a sphere, the curvature is the same everywhere. So the ribs could be divided into repeating segments, cast in series on the site itself, and the whole roof became a problem of manufacturing, not of sculpture. The solution was formalized in the so-called Yellow Book, delivered in January 1962, with plans, sections and the details of the ribs and tiles.

Who had the idea depends on whose version you hear. The Sydney Opera House says Utzon had it alone, one night, in his office in Hellebæk, Denmark, on seeing that the models of the shells nested inside one another, and says the story that he peeled an orange is a myth. The account of engineer Jack Zunz, who took over the problem at Arup in August 1961, describes a joint arrival: the engineers explained that only spherical or toroidal forms gave the repetition the job needed, and the choice came about a month later.

Calculating the shells required one of the first uses of computers in structural analysis. According to Civil Engineering magazine, of the American Society of Civil Engineers, it took about 2,000 hours of computer time up to March 1967.

04Assembling in the air

The builder M. R. Hornibrook took over the shells in 1963. In all, 2,194 precast concrete pieces, of up to 15 tonnes each, were made on site, lifted by cranes and joined to one another with epoxy resin, held together by post-tensioned steel cables. To position them, engineer Joe Bertony, born in Corsica, designed a steel erection arch that moved along the podium. He did the calculations by hand; run again later on an IBM 7090 computer at Woomera, they had no errors.

In Sydney, Arup's resident engineer was Peter Rice, who had worked on the geometry of the shells in London and wrote a program to set out each piece in space. Then came the tiles. The Swedish company Höganäs spent three years developing what became known as the Sydney Tile: 120 millimeters, clay with a little crushed stone, in glossy white and matte cream. There are 1,056,006 tiles, laid in 4,228 chevron-shaped panels, made beneath the monumental staircase.

05The architect who walked away

In 1965 the government changed. The new premier, Robert Askin, had criticized the project, and the Minister for Public Works, Davis Hughes, took control of payments and decisions. With his fees withheld, Utzon resigned on February 28, 1966. He left Australia in April and never returned to see the finished building.

The architect Peter Hall, trained in Sydney, took over the interiors with Lionel Todd and David Littlemore. In December 1966 he decided that the larger hall, which Utzon was designing for both opera and concerts, would be for concerts only, and opera went to a smaller hall; the stage machinery already installed was dismantled. In a 1973 interview, Hall said the auditorium Utzon was working on had seats and aisles that did not meet evacuation rules. The work went on until 1973, with a unique episode in 1972, when the workers threw out the management and worked for about five weeks under their own control.

06The lottery's bill

The 1957 budget, £3.5 million, became AU$ 7 million when Australia swapped the pound for the dollar in 1966. The final cost was AU$ 102 million, 14.6 times the budget, or 1,357% over the estimate, adding up fourteen years of spending with no adjustment for inflation. Instead of four years, it took fourteen, and completion came ten years late.

The bill was paid by gambling. According to the New South Wales Treasury, the Opera House Lottery held 496 draws and sold about 86.7 million tickets, at £5 each, with a top prize of £100,000, and raised AU$ 102 million by 1986. Queen Elizabeth II opened the building on October 20, 1973. Utzon was not there.

07Fifty years of sea

Reconciliation came late. In 1999 Utzon was asked to set out the design principles for the building; in 2003 he received the Pritzker Prize; in 2004 a room redone to his design was named after him. On June 28, 2007, the Opera House was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List, unanimously, on the third attempt. Utzon died in November 2008. The Concert Hall was closed for about two years and reopened in July 2022 with new acoustics.

On August 4, 2026, the state government announced AU$ 120 million for maintenance and upgrades. Besides replacing stage machinery up to 50 years old, the package includes strengthening the structure under the Northern Boardwalk, the platform over the water, after more than half a century in a marine environment. The building that only stood up because its pieces repeated now has to protect the base it rests on.

MYTHSWhat people say and what the record shows

  • What people say

    Utzon peeled an orange and discovered the shells could come from a sphere.

    What the record shows

    The Sydney Opera House itself says the orange story is a myth. The official version is that Utzon saw, one night in 1961, that the models of the shells nested inside one another. The account of engineer Jack Zunz, of Arup, describes reaching the sphere as joint work between architect and engineers.

  • What people say

    The concrete shells would not stand up.

    What the record shows

    What was missing, above all, was geometry. The curves of the 1957 sketch matched no calculable shape, so there was no way to size or fabricate them. The thin shell imagined at the start also could not withstand the wind loads, which led to the ribs. There were 12 schemes, with parabolas and ellipsoids, before the spherical solution.

  • What people say

    Eero Saarinen pulled Utzon's design out of the pile of rejects.

    What the record shows

    It is the most repeated anecdote about the competition, but there is no precise record of how the jury decided. Juror Henry Ashworth denied the story and said that Leslie Martin, another jury member, had already been impressed by the design before Saarinen arrived.

  • What people say

    Nobody died building the Sydney Opera House.

    What the record shows

    No institutional source consulted publishes a death toll for the construction. The claim appears on websites with no source, as does the opposite version, of one death. What exists is an absence of records.

QUESTIONSFrequently asked questions

How was the Sydney Opera House built?

The shells in Utzon's sketch had no calculable geometry; after 12 schemes, in 1961 all of them came to be cut from the surface of a single sphere. That way, 2,194 precast concrete pieces were mass-produced on site, lifted and joined with epoxy resin and post-tensioned steel cables.

When was the Sydney Opera House built and how long did it take?

Construction began on March 2, 1959, and the Opera House was opened by Queen Elizabeth II on October 20, 1973. It took 14 years, against a forecast of 4 years and completion in 1963.

Who was the architect of the Sydney Opera House?

The Dane Jørn Utzon, who won the competition in 1957. He resigned on February 28, 1966, and never returned to see the finished building; the interiors were designed by Peter Hall, with Lionel Todd and David Littlemore. The engineering was by Ove Arup & Partners from start to finish.

How much did the Sydney Opera House cost to build?

The final cost was AU$ 102 million, 14.6 times the 1957 budget of £3.5 million, the same as AU$ 7 million. It was paid for by the Opera House Lottery, run by the New South Wales government, which raised AU$ 102 million by 1986.

How many people died building the Sydney Opera House?

There is no record. No institutional source consulted publishes a number of dead or injured on the job, and the versions in circulation, of no deaths or of one, come from websites with no primary source. About 10,000 workers passed through the job, according to the Sydney Opera House.
