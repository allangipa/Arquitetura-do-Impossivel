Empire State Building

Manhattan, New York, United States · 1930–1931

Putting up 86 floors of steel and stone in 410 days, in the middle of Manhattan, with work starting just months after the stock market crash of 1929.

On 14 August 1930, according to the builder's daily log, 3,439 men were at work on the same block of Manhattan, in New York, United States. The construction of the Empire State Building is one of the best documented of the 20th century: tons of steel erected, dates for every stage, numbered photographs.

And even so, nobody compiled a list of the men who raised it. This page rebuilds the job from what the sources recorded, shows where they disagree, and says plainly what nobody wrote down.

01An announcement, and two months later the crash

At the end of August 1929, Al Smith, former governor of New York, announced the new building. The money came from John J. Raskob and Pierre S. du Pont. The site was that of the Waldorf-Astoria hotel, in Manhattan, and demolition began, according to the New York Times of the day, on 1 October 1929. The 1981 landmark designation report dates the demolition to 1930.

On 24 October 1929, about two months after the announcement, the New York stock market crashed.

The project did not stop. On 13 December 1929, the board of Metropolitan Life approved a loan of $27,500,000 to Empire State, Inc. A month earlier, Al Smith had left the insurer's board precisely because the company he headed was negotiating that loan. The newspaper item still called the building "85 stories": the mast at the top was not yet in the drawings.

02A design that ran alongside the construction

The architect was William F. Lamb, of the firm Shreve, Lamb & Harmon. How long he took to design the building depends on who is telling it. The Smithsonian says two weeks. Lamb himself, in the January 1931 issue of Architectural Forum, described four weeks of study with his team and the basic scheme of the building drawn in a single night, in about two hours.

What the 1930 documents show is a design running only a few weeks ahead of the steel. The first structural drawings were delivered in mid-January 1930. The structural design was only finished up to the slab above the 85th floor in mid-June, when the columns had already been rising for two months.

Work officially began on 17 March 1930, a date engraved on a medallion in the lobby. Steel erection was scheduled for 1 April; photograph No. 133, published by the structural engineer, shows the first columns being set in the basement on 7 April.

03Thirty thousand tons in nine weeks

The steel came from Carnegie Steel, in Homestead, near Pittsburgh. Fabrication was split between two companies, American Bridge, in Ambridge, and McClintic-Marshall, in Rankin, which took turns in groups of six to eight floors. Erection was by Post & McCord. In each of the first two months, 15,000 t were fabricated.

Up top, nine derricks were swinging at the start: five of 30 t and four of 20 t. Between 7 April and 9 June 1930, almost 30,000 t of steel were erected. The structural engineer, J. L. Edwards, wrote in August of that year that this was about 50% more than all the steel in 40 Wall Street or the Chrysler Building, and called the pace a speed record per ton.

There was no yard: the building site was the building itself. More than 200 trucks arrived each day. On every floor, small cars on rails carried material to where it would be used, and there were lunch counters on five floors, so nobody lost time going down.

The pace became a record. The historian John Tauranac records the 26th floor on 20 June and half the frame on 27 July. ENR magazine speaks of 22 floors in 22 working days in July, on regular hours. On average, it came to 4.5 floors a week, when the previous record for a building of that height was 3.5. A first-person sentence, reproduced in books with no confirmed author, says that they once erected 14 and a half floors in 10 working days, "steel, concrete, stone and all"; other historians speak of 14 floors in the same span. The steel reached the top on 19 September 1930, according to the builder's daily log published by Willis and Friedman. The schedule released in 1930 had planned the roof framing for 1 September.

04The steel men

The men up there were, in large part, Irish and Italian immigrants, and a minority of Mohawk ironworkers from Kahnawake, Quebec, Canada. The photographer Lewis Hine followed the work, and some of his photos ran in magazines as early as 1930.

How many there were depends on the count. The builder's daily log recorded 3,439 men on a single day, 14 August 1930. Popular Mechanics, in December 1930, speaks of 5,000 "at the peak of construction"; the Skyscraper Museum, of 3,500; the publisher of Glenn Kurtz's book, of about 3,000; and Kurtz himself, of about 10,000 over the whole job. These are probably different counts — the busiest day and the total number of men who passed through the job — but the sources do not reconcile them.

What they earned also has an official source. Bulletin 540 of the Bureau of Labor Statistics records the New York union scale on 15 May 1930: the structural ironworker received 192.5 cents an hour, $77 for a full week, at 8 hours a day, 40 a week, with Saturdays off and overtime paid double. The bricklayer had the same scale; the bricklayer's helper, 112.5 cents an hour. This is the union's table, not the job's payroll.

Shifts of 12 hours existed, but in the excavation: from January to March 1930, two shifts of 300 men each, according to the historian Langmead (2009). On the demolition of the hotel, an average of 600 men worked.

05Five deaths, and who did the counting

The official number is five, and it has a date and an author. On 22 October 1930, Starrett Brothers & Eken, denying rumors of too many deaths on the job, told the New York Times that five deaths had been recorded: one in the demolition of the Waldorf-Astoria, one in the excavation and three in the construction of the new building. The count is the builder's, a little over six months before the opening.

The historian John Tauranac describes five circumstances: one worker was hit by a truck while sawing a plank; another walked into a blasting area; another stepped off a scaffold; another fell down the elevator shaft; the last was struck by a hoist. By his list, none fell from the steel frame. How that list fits the builder's breakdown (demolition, excavation, tower), the sources do not say.

Other numbers circulated. The New York Daily News spoke of 14 dead. New Masses magazine went as high as "up to 42", a figure historians treat as rumor. And the name of none of the five appears in the available sources.

06The mast, the lights and the empty building

On 1 May 1931, in Washington, President Herbert Hoover pressed a button, and the lights of the Empire State came on in New York. It had been 410 days since 17 March 1930. Counting from the architects' contracts, in September 1929, it was about 20 months. The final cost came to $40,948,900, demolition included — below a budget that the sources give as $50 or $60 million.

The mast at the top was calculated for the force of the wind added to the pull of a moored airship: 318,000 pounds, a little over 7% of the 4,340,000 pounds of wind load on the facade, according to Architectural Forum in August 1930. In practice, it was almost never used. On 15 September 1931, a small US Navy blimp made 25 passes in 45-mile-per-hour winds, dumped ballast and was shaken by the turbulence; the naval historian Norman Polmar identifies it as the J-4 and speaks of a contact of 3 minutes. A New York World-Telegram photo dated 30 September 1931 shows the Goodyear-Zeppelin Columbia trying to pick up mail sacks from the mast: the rope reached Andrew Kelly's hands once, and slipped away. A Universal newsreel of 1 October that year shows, on the contrary, a package delivered into the arms of a tower employee. These may be two different flights.

In the first year, only 23% of the building was rented, and New York started calling it the Empty State Building. In 1936, there was no elevator above the 45th floor. What kept the accounts afloat was the observatory: about 1 million visitors a year, at $1 each, which, according to the landmark designation report, saved the building from bankruptcy, in part. When the first profit came, the sources do not agree: the 1940s or the early 1950s. In 1951, the building was sold for $51 million.

On 28 July 1945, at 9:40 a.m., in fog, a B-25 bomber struck between the 79th and 80th floors. In all, 14 people died: the 3 crew members and 11 people in the building. The elevator operator Betty Lou Oliver fell 75 floors inside an elevator car and survived, a record listed by Guinness.

07Ninety-four years later, the names

For almost a century, the men in Hine's photos remained nameless. The Skyscraper Museum sums it up: no list of workers on this job was ever compiled. The only names left in the building are on a bronze plaque in the lobby, with 32 craftsmen honored by the New York Building Congress — among them Michael Tierney, rock driller, and Vladimir Kozloff, wrecker.

In November 2025, the writer Glenn Kurtz published Men at Work: The Empire State Building and the Untold Story of the Craftsmen Who Built It. Cross-referencing the census, immigration and union records, newspapers of the time and interviews with descendants, the book put names to the men who raised the building. On 1 May 2026, the Empire State turned 95.

MYTHSWhat people say and what the record shows

  • What people say

    The photo of eleven workers eating lunch on a beam was taken at the Empire State.

    What the record shows

    It was not. The photo is from the RCA Building, at Rockefeller Center, in New York, dated 20 September 1932, and it was staged for publicity.

  • What people say

    Airships used to dock at the Empire State's mast.

    What the record shows

    The mast worked as publicity. In September 1931 there was a contact of a few minutes with a small Navy blimp, and flights by a Goodyear airship to pass mail and packages by rope, recorded sometimes as a failure, sometimes as a successful delivery. The famous photo of the docked airship is a composite.

  • What people say

    Hundreds of workers died, and there are men buried in the concrete.

    What the record shows

    The builder recorded 5 deaths up to October 1930, with circumstances described by John Tauranac; none of the circumstances described involves concrete or a fall from the steel frame. The Daily News spoke of 14; New Masses's 42 are treated as rumor.

  • What people say

    The workers earned $1.25 an hour, in 12-hour shifts, seven days a week.

    What the record shows

    The official New York union scale, in May 1930, gave the structural ironworker 192.5 cents an hour, 40 hours a week, Saturdays off and double pay for overtime. Shifts of 12 hours, only in the excavation.

  • What people say

    The whole building was done in 410 days.

    What the record shows

    The number is right, but it runs from 17 March 1930 to 1 May 1931. The architects' contracts date from September 1929 and the demolition of the hotel began on 1 October 1929: from design to opening, it was about 20 months.

QUESTIONSFrequently asked questions

How long did it take to build the Empire State Building?

It took 410 days, from the official start of work, on 17 March 1930, to the opening, on 1 May 1931, at an average of 4.5 floors a week. Counting from the architects' contracts, in September 1929, it was about 20 months.

How many people died building the Empire State Building?

The sources disagree. The builder recorded 5 deaths up to October 1930: 1 in the demolition, 1 in the excavation and 3 on the tower; the New York Daily News spoke of 14, and New Masses magazine's “up to 42” is treated by historians as rumor.

How much did the Empire State Building cost to build?

The final cost was $40,948,900, including the demolition of the Waldorf-Astoria hotel, below a budget that the sources give as $50 or $60 million. Adjusted by the US consumer price index, that is about $867 million in 2025 dollars.

How many workers built the Empire State Building?

The sources disagree. The builder's daily log recorded 3,439 men on a single day, 14 August 1930; other counts range from about 3,000 to 5,000 at the peak, and Glenn Kurtz speaks of about 10,000 over the whole job.

How tall is the Empire State Building?

It is 381 m to the 102nd floor and 443 m to the tip of the antenna. The 102 floors are 86 floors, plus the mast counted as 14, plus 2 basement levels.
