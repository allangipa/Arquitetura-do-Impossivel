Channel Tunnel

Under the Strait of Dover, between Folkestone, Kent, England, and Coquelles, Pas-de-Calais, France · 1987–1994

Bore three tunnels 50 kilometers long, almost 40 of them under the sea, from two coasts and two countries with different maps, following a single layer of soft rock, so that the machines would meet in the middle without any government money.

The English began digging a tunnel to France in 1880, from the cliffs of Dover in the county of Kent, and stopped for fear of invasion. They tried again in 1974 and stopped because of the cost. The third time, between 1987 and 1994, no government put in a single penny: the treaty between the two countries forbade it.

What made the project possible was a band of rock. Between the hard chalk above and the clay below, beneath the seabed, runs a layer of chalk marl, soft enough to be cut and watertight enough to hold back the water of the Channel. Eleven tunnel boring machines followed that layer, six setting out from England and five from France, and on 1 December 1990 the two ends of the service tunnel met 22.3 kilometers from the English coast.

01A hundred years of abandoned tunnels

The first serious dig began in 1880. A machine driven by compressed air, designed by Captain Thomas English and credited to Colonel Frederick Beaumont, cut through the chalk from Abbot's Cliff and Shakespeare Cliff, near Dover, in England. By 1882, the Shakespeare Cliff heading was almost two kilometers long, and the French were digging theirs from Sangatte, near Calais. The work stopped out of a fear that had nothing geological about it: in the British press and Parliament, the tunnel became the front door for an enemy army.

The next attempt came almost a century later. Edward Heath and Georges Pompidou signed a treaty in 1973, and work restarted at Shakespeare Cliff in 1974. On 20 January 1975, minister Anthony Crosland told the House of Commons that Harold Wilson's government was giving up. The rail link on the English side had become too expensive, and abandoning the project would cost the taxpayer about £20 million. How much was dug that time the sources do not agree: some speak of 250 meters of trial tunnel, others of one mile.

02Not a penny of government money

When the subject came back, in the 1980s, Margaret Thatcher imposed a condition. The Treaty of Canterbury, signed with François Mitterrand on 12 February 1986, states in Article 1 that the tunnel will be financed without recourse to government funds or guarantees. According to Michael Grant, who handled Eurotunnel's finances, it was the only basis on which Thatcher would accept the project.

Four proposals reached the final round: a suspension bridge, a bridge-and-tunnel combination, four tunnels carrying road and rail, and Eurotunnel's, with three rail-only tunnels, in which cars and trucks travel inside the train. Budgeted at £2.6 billion, a little above the rival four-tunnel proposal and well below the two bridges, it was chosen in January 1986. Two of the tunnels carry the trains, one in each direction. The third, smaller, runs between them: it is the service tunnel, used by maintenance and rescue crews, and the refuge for anyone who has to leave a stopped train.

03A band of chalk under the sea

The route was not chosen on the map, it was chosen in the rock. Surveys carried out since the nineteenth century, more than a hundred boreholes in the seabed and about a thousand kilometers of seismic lines revealed a layer of chalk marl: a limestone rock with clay, weak, uniform and practically watertight. Above it lies the hard, fractured chalk; below it, clay. The tunnels run about 40 meters below the seabed, following that layer.

On the English side it is well behaved. On the French side, in the first kilometers under the sea, the chalk is cracked and waterlogged. That is why the two shores used different machines. The six British tunnel boring machines were open-faced. The five French ones were closed, with the cutting chamber sealed to withstand 11 bar of water pressure, and the excavated chalk passed through an airlock before reaching the wagons. At Sangatte, away from the beach, the French sank a watertight shaft about 50 meters wide and more than 60 deep, from which all their machines set out.

Two thirds of the tunnel were bored from England and one third from France. The three tunnels together add up to about 150 kilometers, bored 24 hours a day, and the alignment tolerance required of the builders was 15 centimeters.

04The meeting of 1 December 1990

The service tunnel went first, as a probe for the other two. The surveyors had an invisible problem: the British and French maps used different projection systems, and not even sea level matched on the two coasts. After the breakthrough, the measured difference between the sea levels of the two coasts was about 36 centimeters.

The two machines stopped 100 meters from each other. The offset was measured: 358 millimeters sideways and 58 in height, according to the French builders' technical sheet, or 361 and 58, according to a German measurement published in 1991. The English machine advanced another 50 meters on a corrected line, was turned off into a tight curve and buried right there; the French one was dismantled, and the last stretch was dug with hand tools.

On 1 December 1990, at 12:12, 22.3 kilometers from England and 15.6 from France, Briton Graham Fagg and Frenchman Philippe Cozette shook hands through the opening. The rail tunnels met on 22 May and 28 June 1991. So that the machines would not collide head-on, each English tunnel boring machine was steered down below the floor and entombed in concrete, and the French one passed over it.

05How many worked and how many died

The operator speaks of more than 13,000 workers at the peak, recruited mainly in the regions on both sides of the Channel; other sources speak of 15,000. The French site alone reached 5,234 people, counting employees and subcontractors, in August 1990.

The death toll also has two versions. The most repeated is ten, eight of them British, most in the first months of boring. The plaque that the operator and the contractor put up at Samphire Hoe, by the sea in Dover, bears eleven names, with dates from 1986 to 1992. Two of them are French: Guy Joly, 29, killed in August 1989 at kilometer 4.1 of the service tunnel under the sea, and René Saint-Georges, 56, struck by a vehicle in the yard of the lining-segment factory in May 1990. In October 1990, the contractor's safety manager stated that the French side had at least five dead, three of them undisclosed, because France recorded accidents in a different way.

Samphire Hoe is itself a piece of the project: a site of about 30 hectares made from 4.9 million cubic meters of the chalk excavated on the English side, at the foot of the cliffs. On the French side, the chalk slurry was pumped into a valley behind a dam.

06The bill that blew up

At 1985 prices, the tunnel budgeted at £2.6 billion cost £4.65 billion, about 80% more, according to the assessment of economist Richard Anguera. In 1994 pounds, that comes to nearly £9.5 billion. By the British Retail Prices Index, the £4.65 billion of 1985 are worth something like £20 billion in 2025.

According to the company itself, the tunnel boring machines advanced more slowly than expected at the start, the logistics were underestimated and the trains cost far more than anticipated. In 1992, the contractor claimed about £1.5 billion more, and Eurotunnel had to pay £50 million a month while the arbitration was pending. There were five share issues and a loan syndicated among more than 200 banks. In September 1995, little more than a year after the opening, the company suspended interest payments. Restructurings followed in 1997 and in 2006–2007, the latter under the protection of a Paris court, with the debt cut from about €9 billion to about €4 billion. In Anguera's view, the British economy would have been better off if the tunnel had never been built.

07Fire, border and a man on foot

Queen Elizabeth II and President Mitterrand opened the tunnel on 6 May 1994. Two years later, on 18 November 1996, a truck shuttle caught fire on its way to England, with 34 people on board; no one died. On 11 September 2008, another fire burned for about 16 hours and forced the replacement of 650 meters of lining. In the summer of 2015, migrants tried to get into the Coquelles terminal by the hundreds and, on one night in July, between 1,500 and 2,000 times; about ten died near the terminal within a few weeks. Since Brexit, freight has gone through customs again.

On 30 December 2025, a power supply failure and a stalled train closed the tunnel on the eve of the New Year, and Eurostar canceled its trains to and from London that day. In June 2026, the operator turned down a request from Briton Karl Bushby, who had been walking from Chile since 1998, to cross the Channel on foot through the service tunnel: closing it for 15 hours, the company said, would put passenger trains and maintenance at risk. The tunnel of the 1990 handshake is still, every day, the emergency exit for the other two.

MYTHSWhat people say and what the record shows

  • What people say

    The tunnel was a public works project of the British and French governments.

    What the record shows

    The 1986 treaty forbids government money and guarantees. The project was paid for by shareholders and by a loan from more than 200 banks, and the company spent more than a decade renegotiating its debt, from 1995 to 2007.

  • What people say

    The two sides met with an error of just a few centimeters.

    What the record shows

    The offset measured in the service tunnel was about 36 centimeters sideways and 58 millimeters in height, after 22.3 km on one side and 15.6 km on the other. That is remarkable precision, but not a few centimeters, and the "58 centimeters" that sometimes appears for the height is a unit error.

  • What people say

    Ten workers died building it.

    What the record shows

    Ten is the most cited count, with eight British. The plaque in memory of the workers, in Dover, has eleven names, and the contractor's safety manager said in 1990 that there had been deaths on the French side that had not been disclosed.

  • What people say

    It cost £4.65 billion. Or £9.5. Or more than 10.

    What the record shows

    All three figures circulate and they measure different things: the cost at 1985 prices, the same cost in 1994 pounds, and the total including financing. At constant prices, the overrun on the 1985 budget was about 80%.

  • What people say

    The English never wanted the tunnel.

    What the record shows

    They dug in 1880 and in 1974. The first time they stopped for fear of a military invasion; the second, because of the cost.

QUESTIONSFrequently asked questions

When was the Channel Tunnel built?

Between 1987 and 1994. Boring began in December 1987 on the English side and on 28 February 1988 on the French side; the service tunnel broke through on 1 December 1990, and it opened on 6 May 1994.

How long and how deep is the Channel Tunnel?

Each of the three tunnels is 50 km long, about 38 of them under the sea. They run about 40 m below the seabed, following a layer of chalk marl between the hard chalk above and the clay below.

How was the Channel Tunnel built underwater?

Eleven tunnel boring machines, six from England and five from France, dug two rail tunnels and a service tunnel between them, following a soft, watertight layer of chalk marl. When the two halves of the service tunnel met, the offset was about 36 cm sideways and 58 mm in height.

How many workers died building the Channel Tunnel?

Accounts differ: the most cited count is 10 dead, 8 of them British, but the plaque in memory of the workers in Dover bears 11 names. In 1990, the contractor's safety manager said there had been at least five deaths on the French side, three of them undisclosed.

How much did the Channel Tunnel cost to build?

Budgeted at £2.6 billion in 1985, it ended at £4.65 billion at the same 1985 prices, an overrun of about 80%. In 1994 pounds, about £9.5 billion, paid with private money only, because the treaty forbade government funds.
