Eiffel Tower

Champ-de-Mars, Paris, Île-de-France, France · 1887–1889

Raising 300 meters of iron, nearly twice the height of the tallest structure in the world, in just over two years, with its shape dictated by the wind calculations, and opening it in time for the Universal Exposition of 1889.

Until 1889, the tallest structure in the world was the Washington Monument, at about 169 meters. For that year's Universal Exposition, on the Champ-de-Mars in Paris, France, Gustave Eiffel's company promised a 300-meter iron tower, assembled in just over two years and paid for mostly by Eiffel and by a company formed with banks.

The design was not Eiffel's. It came, in June 1884, from two engineers at the firm, Maurice Koechlin and Émile Nouguier, and took its final form with the architect Stephen Sauvestre. Eiffel bought the patent, took on the risk and gave it his name. The curve of the four legs, he himself explained in 1887, was the one that the calculation of wind resistance had given him.

01A bridge pier stretched to 300 meters

Gustave Eiffel's company, in Levallois-Perret, on the outskirts of Paris, made its living from iron bridges and viaducts. In June 1884, with the Universal Exposition of 1889 on the horizon, two of the firm's engineers, Maurice Koechlin, head of the design office, and Émile Nouguier, had the idea of stretching to 300 meters the kind of lattice pier the company already knew how to build. The sketch, signed by both and dated 6 June 1884, shows four lattice girders that spread apart at the base and meet at the top, with Notre-Dame, the Statue of Liberty and the Arc de Triomphe stacked alongside for scale.

According to the company that operates the tower, the two then called in the firm's architect, Stephen Sauvestre. He added a masonry base, linked the four legs to the first floor with great arches, and drew glazed halls and a bulb-shaped top. Much of that was later simplified; the arches stayed. On 18 September 1884, Eiffel filed the patent in all three names. At the end of that year, he bought out Koechlin's and Nouguier's rights. According to the Koechlin family, the deal was 1% of what Eiffel received for the work for each of them and a promise to mention their names. The tower got his.

02The shape the wind drew

A 300-meter tower does not fall because of its weight: it falls because of the wind. The weight, spread over the four legs, put a pressure of only a few kilograms per square centimeter on the ground. The problem was the sideways push, which grows with height and tries to topple the tower like a lever. The answer was to give the four legs a curve calculated so that, at every height, the force of the wind would run inside the edges themselves, without any need for diagonals crossing the gap between them. And to leave everything open: an open lattice offers little surface for the wind to push against.

Eiffel said so in public on 14 February 1887, in the newspaper Le Temps, replying to the artists who were attacking the tower: the condition he had had to deal with above all was resistance to the wind, and the curves of the four edges, just as the calculation had produced them, would give an impression of beauty. In a text quoted today by the company that operates the tower, he wrote that the legs seemed to spring from the ground and to have been molded by the action of the wind.

03Eighteen thousand pieces, each with its own drawing

The agreement of 8 January 1887 was signed by minister Édouard Lockroy, by the prefect of the Seine, Eugène Poubelle, and by Eiffel. Until the Exposition opened, in May 1889, there were just over two years. The foundations began at the end of that month. In five months the masonry blocks that receive each edge were built; on the Seine side, below the water table, the workers dug inside compressed-air metal caissons. On 1 July 1887 the ironwork assembly began.

The secret of the speed lay far from the Champ-de-Mars. At the Levallois-Perret factory, about 50 engineers and draftsmen produced thousands of drawings for the 18,038 pieces of puddled iron that came from the Pompey forges, in Lorraine. Each piece was marked out, cut and drilled there, and pre-assembled in sections of some 5 meters. On site, between 150 and 300 men, according to the company that operates the tower, hoisted the sections with small steam cranes that climbed along the rails of the future elevators, bolted them temporarily and replaced the bolts with hot-driven rivets. According to the company that operates the tower, each rivet took four men: one heated it, one held it, one formed the head and one struck it with the sledgehammer.

The most delicate moment was the first floor. The four legs rose at an angle, resting on wooden scaffolds of 30 and then 45 meters, and the great girders of the floor had to meet them in exactly the right place. The angle of each leg was adjusted with sandboxes, emptied little by little, and hydraulic jacks at the base. The junction was made on 7 December 1887. From then on, the tower served as its own building site: second floor in mid-1888 (July or August, depending on the source), top at the end of March 1889.

04Five centimes an hour

On 19 September 1888, with the second floor finished, the workers stopped. According to a CGT survey of the archives of the Paris Prefecture of Police, only 27 of the usual 140 or so turned up that day. A riveter earned 0.70 francs an hour; a carpenter, 0.80. Eiffel granted a raise of five centimes an hour and a bonus for the longest-serving men when the tower reached the lantern platform, but refused to pay more for height. In December there was another strike; the sources disagree on whether or not Eiffel negotiated.

Nobody recorded how many men passed through the job. The company that operates the tower says that none died on the job, in work done on small platforms, without protection, in the cold and the wind, and that an Italian worker died outside working hours, his widow discreetly compensated by Eiffel. The documents of that compensation agreement, held at the Musée d'Orsay, give a name and a date: Angelo Scagliotti, killed in an accident on 24 May 1889 on the tower site. It was a Friday, nine days after the opening to the public. According to the family's research, which reproduces the death certificate and the agreement, he was crushed by the car during the installation of the first elevator, and the agreement says he died working on the site on Eiffel's account.

05The protest, the cost and the celebration

The work had barely begun when Le Temps published, on 14 February 1887, the letter from dozens of artists to the director of works of the Exposition, Adolphe Alphand: Charles Garnier, the architect of the Opéra, the composer Charles Gounod, Guy de Maupassant, Leconte de Lisle, among others. They called the tower useless and monstrous and protested, above all, against keeping it standing for twenty years. Eiffel replied in the same issue: nobody had seen the tower yet, only a drawing, and the colossal had a charm of its own, like the pyramids.

The final bill came to 7,799,401.31 francs, about 1.3 million more than the estimated 6.5 million and more than double Eiffel's first budget. The State paid 1.5 million, in three installments tied to the first floor, the second floor and completion; the rest came from Eiffel and from a company formed with three banks. On 31 March 1889 the flag was raised at the top and Eiffel received the Legion of Honor up there. During the Exposition, about 2 million people went up the tower.

06Saved by the antenna

Once the Exposition was over, the crowds dwindled. The agreement gave Eiffel the right to operate the tower for 20 years from 1 January 1890; after that, the tower, which already belonged to the City of Paris, would revert to it entirely, and nobody could promise the City would want it. In 1906, Paris decided to demolish the Galerie des Machines, another giant of 1889, for lack of use.

Eiffel looked for a scientific use. In 1898, Eugène Ducretet made the tower one end of a wireless telegraphy link with the Panthéon, 4 km away. From 1903, Eiffel paid for the antenna for the experiments of Captain Gustave Ferrié, of the Army. The following year, according to the company that operates the tower, the range reached 400 km, and in 1908 6,000 km; in 1909 it gained a permanent station under the Champ-de-Mars. The concession was extended for 70 years from 1 January 1910. In the First World War, the tower intercepted German messages. Eiffel had already left business: convicted in 1893 in the scandal of the French Panama canal, whose locks his company had contracted to build in 1887, he had the conviction overturned by the Court of Cassation the same year, on the statute of limitations.

07Iron that needs paint

Eiffel wrote, in a much-quoted line, that the tower had been made so that every part could be reached and examined for the first sign of rust. The iron of 1889 is still standing only because it is repainted in full from time to time, with about 60 tonnes of paint per campaign. The 20th campaign began in 2019, costs more than 80 million euros, has had to deal with the lead in the old paints and is giving the tower back the yellowish brown that Eiffel chose in 1907; as of September 2026, it was scheduled to be completed that year, with the inner face.

In September 2026, the tower stopped for another reason. On a Saturday, 5 September, management removed female employees from their posts during the private visit of a religious delegation, at the visitors' request. On Monday the 7th, the staff went on strike and the tower stayed closed all day, reopening the next day. The company that operates it acknowledged that it should not have accepted the request, and on 29 September the managing director announced that he would leave the post at the end of 2026.

MYTHSWhat people say and what the record shows

  • What people say

    Gustave Eiffel designed the tower.

    What the record shows

    The idea and the first drawing, of 6 June 1884, belong to the engineers Émile Nouguier and Maurice Koechlin; the architectural form is Stephen Sauvestre's. Eiffel filed the patent in all three names, bought out the two engineers' rights at the end of 1884, and financed, built and operated the tower.

  • What people say

    The contract required the tower to be dismantled after 20 years.

    What the record shows

    The agreement of 8 January 1887 says nothing about dismantling. It says that the City of Paris becomes the owner of the tower after the Exposition and that, once Eiffel's 20 years of operation, counted from 1 January 1890, are over, operation reverts to the City. Demolition was a real risk, not a clause.

  • What people say

    Nobody died during construction. Or, at the opposite extreme, more than 300 workers died.

    What the record shows

    The 300 deaths are false, as an AFP fact-check showed in 2022. The version of the company that operates the tower is no deaths on the job. The documents held at the Musée d'Orsay record the death of Angelo Scagliotti in an accident on the site, on 24 May 1889, while the elevators were being installed.

  • What people say

    Émile Zola signed the artists' protest against the tower.

    What the record shows

    Zola's name is not among the signatures published by Le Temps on 14 February 1887. Those of Charles Garnier, Gounod, Maupassant, Leconte de Lisle, Sully Prudhomme, Victorien Sardou, Meissonier and others are.

  • What people say

    The tower is made of cast iron.

    What the record shows

    It is made of puddled iron, a wrought iron, produced by the Dupont et Fould forges, in Pompey, Lorraine.

QUESTIONSFrequently asked questions

Who designed the Eiffel Tower?

The first drawing, of 6 June 1884, is by two engineers at Gustave Eiffel's company, Maurice Koechlin and Émile Nouguier; the architect Stephen Sauvestre gave it its final form, with the great arches at the base. Eiffel filed the patent in all three names, bought out the two engineers' rights, and financed and built the tower.

When was the Eiffel Tower built?

Between 1887 and 1889, for the Paris Universal Exposition. The foundations began at the end of January 1887, the ironwork assembly on 1 July 1887, and the top was reached on 31 March 1889: 2 years, 2 months and 5 days. The tower opened to the public on 15 May 1889.

How was the Eiffel Tower built?

The 18,038 pieces of puddled iron were drawn, drilled and pre-assembled in sections of about 5 meters at the Levallois-Perret factory and joined on site with hot-driven rivets. The first floor rested on wooden scaffolds, and the angle of the legs was adjusted with sandboxes and hydraulic jacks.

How many people died building the Eiffel Tower?

The accounts differ. The company that operates the tower speaks of no deaths on the job; documents held at the Musée d'Orsay record the death of Angelo Scagliotti in an accident on the site, on 24 May 1889, during the installation of the elevators. The story of more than 300 workers killed is false.

How much did the Eiffel Tower cost to build?

7,799,401.31 francs of 1889, against 6.5 million estimated at the time of the agreement. The State put in 1.5 million; the rest came from Eiffel and from a company formed with three banks.
