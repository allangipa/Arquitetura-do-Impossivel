Golden Gate Bridge

Golden Gate strait, between San Francisco and Marin County, California, United States · 1933–1937

Planting a steel tower in the open sea, at the mouth of a bay swept by tidal currents and fog, and hanging from it the longest suspension span in the world, with cables spun wire by wire on site.

  • 1933–1937from the start of work on 5 January 1933 to the opening on 27 and 28 May 19372+ sources
  • 27,572steel wires in each of the two main cables, spun on site2+ sources

The Golden Gate Bridge does not cross a river. It crosses the only opening from San Francisco Bay, in California, in the United States, to the Pacific Ocean: a strait carved into the rock, more than 100 meters deep at its deepest point, with tides that rush in and out and fog that rolls in from the sea without warning. It was there, in the depths of the Great Depression, with one in four American workers unemployed, that it was decided to hang a roadway with a 1,280-meter span.

It is the third suspension bridge in this series, after the Brooklyn and the Hercílio Luz, and the boldest of the three in size. But its story has two problems that are not in the drawing: a tower that had to be born in the open sea, protected by a concrete bathtub, and an engineer who did the bridge's math and only had his name recognized seventy years later.

01The mouth of the bay

The name Golden Gate is older than the bridge and older than the gold rush: according to the district that manages the bridge, it was given to the strait by the explorer John C. Frémont in 1846. It is the passage through which the tide fills and empties San Francisco Bay, with strong currents. The US Geological Survey measures there a channel carved into the rock up to 113 meters deep; summer fog often covers the mouth of the bay; and the open sea beats straight against the shores.

That is why the crossing was made by ferry, and a bridge there was a matter for visionaries. The city engineer, Michael O'Shaughnessy, began a study in 1919. In 1921, Joseph B. Strauss, a builder of bascule bridges, presented a design: a hybrid of two cantilever arms with a suspended section in the middle, estimated at about $17 million. The press found it ugly. It would take another eight years, a state law, the creation of a district with six counties and the permission of the War Department, owner of both shores, for Strauss to be appointed chief engineer, in 1929.

02The name on the plaque and the name behind the math

The bridge that exists is not the 1921 design. Leon S. Moisseiff, a New York engineer, persuaded Strauss to drop the hybrid for a pure suspension bridge, and brought in deflection theory, which calculates how cable and deck deform together and allows a lighter, more slender deck. The man who turned this into numbers was Charles A. Ellis, a former engineering professor at the University of Illinois, hired by Strauss in 1922. In 1930, in four months of work, Ellis completed the design and the estimates; PBS, citing the historian Kevin Starr, speaks of ten and a half volumes of calculations done by hand.

In 1931, before construction began, Strauss removed Ellis, accusing him of wasting time and money, and handed the final design to his assistant Clifford Paine. According to PBS, Ellis's name disappeared from the bridge's official material for more than 50 years, and the one who got the statue by the toll plaza was Strauss. Only in 2007, on the 70th anniversary of the opening, did the district itself acknowledge in a report that the main role in the design had been Ellis's; in 2012, on the 75th anniversary, a plaque on the south tower began to say so.

The visible design has a third author. The architect Irving F. Morrow drew the lampposts, the railing and the vertical ribbing of the towers, and chose the color. According to the district, he was inspired by the reddish red-lead primer on the steel as it arrived from Bethlehem Steel, seen from the ferry he took to the site, and he rejected black, gray and aluminum. The Navy wanted the bridge striped in black and yellow, so that ships could see it in the fog. The orange stayed.

03A bathtub in the open sea

The north tower was set in shallow water, close to the Marin shore, and by June 1934 it was already standing. The south tower was another problem: its site was in the open sea, about 335 meters from the San Francisco beach. To get there, the builders made a wooden trestle on piles. In August 1933, a freighter leaving the bay in thick fog ran into it and destroyed a section; two months after it was repaired, according to the district, more than half of the structure collapsed in a storm.

The solution was to enclose the pier site with a fender, an oval ring of concrete placed underwater through pipes into wooden forms, which the workers nicknamed the bathtub. Once the ring was closed, the water inside was pumped out, about 9.4 million gallons by the district's count, and the pier was built in the dry. Before that, divers had worked at depths of up to 110 feet. On top of it, the steel south tower rose in six months and was finished in June 1935. The fender is still there, protecting the base of the tower against ship collisions.

04Wire by wire across the strait

The cables did not arrive ready-made. They were spun in place by John A. Roebling's Sons Company, the Trenton, New Jersey, firm founded by John A. Roebling, the same engineer who designed the Brooklyn Bridge. On the Hercílio Luz, in Florianópolis, the bridge hung from chains of steel eyebars; here, the technique was the Brooklyn's taken to the limit: spinning wheels traveled back and forth over temporary catwalks, from shore to shore, pulling, wire after wire, strands about five millimeters thick.

Each of the two main cables ended up with 27,572 wires, grouped into 61 strands and compacted into a cylinder 92 centimeters in diameter. All together, the wire adds up to 80,000 miles, about 129,000 kilometers. The spinning began in October 1935 and ended in May 1936, ahead of schedule. In November of that year, the two halves of the deck met in the middle of the span. A Navy photograph of 12 November 1936 shows three aircraft carriers passing beneath the still-open bridge.

05The net and the eleven

Strauss imposed rules that were not common on bridge jobs: hard hats, glare-proof goggles, cream against the wind, a diet against dizziness. And he had a safety net strung, at a cost of $130,000, under the entire work, wider and longer than the deck itself. It caught 19 men who fell. They began calling themselves the Halfway to Hell Club, the club of those who fell only halfway to hell.

Until February 1937 there had been one death, and it was not a fall: on 21 October 1936, the ironworker Kermit Moore was struck when a crane toppled. Then, three months before the opening, a moving scaffold that ran beneath the deck broke loose with the men working on it and plunged onto the net, which was not made for that weight and gave way. Ten died; two survived, according to PBS. The district dates the accident to 17 February 1937; PBS, to the 16th. No official figure records how many men worked on the job in all.

06A debt paid with tolls

The bridge was paid for by the people of the region, and at their own risk. On 4 November 1930, voters in six counties approved $35 million in bonds, pledging homes, farms and businesses in the district as collateral. The bonds sat unsold through the Depression and lawsuits until 1932, when A. P. Giannini's Bank of America bought 3 million of them and unblocked the sale. The work ended with a surplus of about $1.3 million.

The bill went to the toll: 50 cents per car in 1937, according to PBS. On 27 May 1937, Pedestrian Day, the bridge opened only to those on foot; the next day, at noon, President Franklin Roosevelt pressed a telegraph key in Washington and the cars crossed. The last bond was redeemed in 1971: $35 million in principal and almost 39 million in interest, all paid by those who crossed.

07Ninety-three years later, back at the south tower

On 24 October 2025, the district approved the first contract of the final phase of the bridge's seismic retrofit, with the contractor Halmar International, of New York, for $863.9 million. The notice to proceed took effect on 5 January 2026, 93 years after the day the original work began, and the field work, with the mobilization of the work sites, began in May 2026. The contract calls for strengthening the two towers, the trusses, the side spans and the south tower pier, with completion expected in 2031; the whole phase runs until 2036.

Adjusted only by the US consumer price index, which measures consumer prices and not construction costs, the $35 million of 1933 are equivalent to about $870 million in 2025 dollars: by coincidence, almost the value of this contract, which strengthens a bridge that is already built. Meanwhile, the toll went up 50 cents on 1 July 2026 and reached $10.25 with FasTrak, charged southbound only. Beneath the sidewalks, since 2024, runs another steel net, installed to prevent suicides, the second net in the history of this bridge.

MYTHSWhat people say and what the record shows

  • What people say

    Joseph Strauss designed the Golden Gate Bridge.

    What the record shows

    Strauss was the chief engineer and the promoter of the project. His 1921 design was an abandoned hybrid. The calculations for the bridge that exists are by Charles Ellis, with Leon Moisseiff's theory, and the district itself acknowledged Ellis's main role in 2007; the plaque came in 2012.

  • What people say

    The orange color was chosen so ships could see the bridge.

    What the record shows

    The one that wanted a signal color was the Navy, which asked for black and yellow stripes. International Orange came out of a study by the architect Irving Morrow, inspired by the reddish primer on the steel, because it blended with the hills and contrasted with the sea and the sky.

  • What people say

    The bridge is painted from end to end every year, and when the painters finish they start over.

    What the record shows

    No. According to the district, painting is continuous maintenance, done section by section by a permanent crew of painters, and not a complete pass every year.

  • What people say

    The safety net prevented deaths on the job.

    What the record shows

    It saved 19 men who fell. But it was precisely through the net that the ten men killed in February 1937 fell: the scaffold that broke loose tore it apart. In all, 11 workers died.

  • What people say

    The name Golden Gate comes from the color of the bridge or from the gold rush.

    What the record shows

    According to the district, the strait was named by John C. Frémont in 1846, before the gold rush and almost a century before the bridge.

QUESTIONSFrequently asked questions

When was the Golden Gate Bridge built?

Between 1933 and 1937. Work began on 5 January 1933, the main span was joined in November 1936, and the bridge opened to pedestrians on 27 May 1937 and to vehicles on 28 May 1937.

Who designed the Golden Gate Bridge?

Joseph Strauss was the chief engineer, but the calculations for the bridge that exists are by Charles Ellis, with Leon Moisseiff's deflection theory; the architect Irving Morrow drew the Art Deco details and chose the color. Ellis's main role was only acknowledged by the district in 2007.

How long is the Golden Gate Bridge?

The main span is 1,280 m, the longest suspension span in the world from 1937 to 1964. The total length is about 2,737 m, the towers rise 227 m above the water and the roadway sits 67 m above the water.

How many workers died building the Golden Gate Bridge?

Eleven workers. Kermit Moore died on 21 October 1936, struck when a crane fell; the other ten died in February 1937, when a moving scaffold broke loose and tore through the safety net. The same net saved another 19 men who fell.

How much did the Golden Gate Bridge cost to build?

US$35 million in bonds approved by voters in 1930, of which about US$27.1 million went to the construction proper. The debt was paid off in 1971 by tolls alone: 35 million in principal and almost 39 million in interest.
