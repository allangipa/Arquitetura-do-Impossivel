Structure 05 · Brazil

Itaipu Dam

Paraná River, between Foz do Iguaçu, Brazil, and Hernandarias, Paraguay · 1975–1984

Taking the Paraná River out of its own bed to raise, on dry ground, a 196 m high dam owned in equal shares by two countries.

In October 1982, the gates of a dam on the Paraná River were closed. In 14 days, the water rose until it covered the falls of Sete Quedas, one of the largest waterfalls in the world by volume of water. Four years earlier, the same builders had done the opposite: taken the river out of its bed so they could work in the dry.

The Itaipu Dam stands between Foz do Iguaçu, in Brazil, and Hernandarias, in Paraguay, and by treaty it belongs half to each country. This page tells how it was raised, who stood in its way and what still has no firm number today.

01A border on top of a waterfall

The problem began as a dispute over a map. In the 1960s, Brazil and Paraguay were disputing the area of the Sete Quedas falls, between Guaíra, in Paraná, Brazil, and Salto del Guairá, in Paraguay. The way out came on 22 June 1966: the Act of Iguaçu, negotiated from Porto Presidente Stroessner (today Ciudad del Este, Paraguay) and signed in Foz do Iguaçu, Brazil, traded the border quarrel for a joint project, harnessing the Paraná River.

Seven years later, on 26 April 1973, in Brasília, the foreign ministers Mário Gibson Barboza and Raúl Sapena Pastor signed the Treaty of Itaipu. The text creates an entity with two owners: Itaipu Binacional, incorporated on 17 May 1974, with capital of 100 million dollars divided into equal, non-transferable shares between Eletrobras and Paraguay's ANDE. The energy would also be split down the middle, and each country would have the right to buy whatever the other did not use.

The split reached the machines. Paraguay uses 50-hertz current; Brazil, 60. That is why the plant has 20 units of 700 megawatts, half turning at one rate and half at the other, for a total of 14,000 megawatts.

02Moving the river

A dam is not built inside the current. To work on the bed of the Paraná River, the engineers opened a diversion channel on the bank: about 2 km long, 150 m wide and 90 m deep, according to Itaipu. Two cofferdams, concrete arches, held the water out of the channel while it was being dug.

On 20 October 1978, 58 tons of explosives brought down the two cofferdams, and the river began to run along the new path. Presidents Ernesto Geisel and Alfredo Stroessner met in the middle of the Friendship Bridge and threw the switch together. With the bed dry, work on the dam proper began.

In the end, the job excavated more than 50 million cubic meters of earth and rock and used more than 12 million cubic meters of concrete. Itaipu itself publishes three figures for the concrete, 12.3, 12.6 or 12.7 million, without explaining the difference. The dam, adding up its concrete, rockfill and earth sections, reaches 7,919 m in length and 196 m in height.

03The building site

How many men worked at the same time depends on who is counting. The builders' records, cited by the historians Pedro Henrique Pedreira Campos and Valdir Sessi and by Agência Pública, give almost 32,000 on the site in 1978. Itaipu speaks of about 40,000, adding the site and the support offices in both countries. With turnover, more than 100,000 people passed through the job.

The project was also a divided city. In Foz do Iguaçu, Vila A housed technicians and administrative staff; Vila B, senior management; Vila C, the least skilled workers. Many were not career dam builders, but farmhands from the region brought in by recruiters.

And it was watched. Academic research documents that Itaipu's Special Security and Information Office, present on both sides of the border, spied on workers and exchanged information with the dictatorship's intelligence and repression agencies, such as the SNI and the DOPS. The plant's guards wore brown, and the workers called them the Brown Guard. Strikes only gained strength after the local union was founded, in 1986; in those of 1987 there were troops and military vehicles on the site.

04Counting the dead

There is no official death toll for the whole job. What Itaipu Binacional reported, in writing, to Agência Pública in 2023 was an estimate: 43,530 workplace accidents between 1978 and 1984, Brazilian and Paraguayan, of which 106 were fatal. For 1975 to 1977 and for 1985 to 1991, when the machines were still being installed, the company gave no number.

Other counts exist, all partial. The 2004 thesis by the historian Ivone Lima reaches 40 deaths between 1975 and 1979; an internal bulletin of the Unicon consortium totals 56 between 1976 and 1981, on the Brazilian bank alone; the journalist Tão Gomes Pinto speaks of 138. A former safety technician on the job told Pública that his team counted up to 800, in a survey that was lost. An anonymous Itaipu source told the EFE news agency, in 2013, of about a thousand, adding accidents and occupational illnesses.

Among the cases with a date is that of 17 December 1981: an accident with a Peiner crane, during maintenance of its boom, killed 5 workers. Researchers also recorded accounts of victims taken to the hospital so that the death would not be listed as having occurred on the site, which made compensation harder.

05Fourteen days

Sete Quedas — "Seven Falls" — were not seven falls. There were 18 or 19, depending on the source, arranged in seven groups, with a cited flow of about 13,300 m³ per second. The National Park that protected them was abolished on 4 July 1981. On 17 January 1982, an overcrowded footbridge over one of the falls collapsed and killed 32 tourists.

The farewell was public. In July 1982, according to the Paraná press, environmental groups gathered thousands of people in the park, at the Quarup Adeus Sete Quedas. On 9 September, Carlos Drummond de Andrade published the column "Adeus a Sete Quedas" (Farewell to Sete Quedas) in the Jornal do Brasil.

On 13 October 1982, the gates of the diversion channel were closed. Helped by a flood, the water filled the reservoir and, by 27 October, had covered the falls. On the same day as the closing, Operation Mymba Kuera began — Guarani for "the animals" — which by 10 January 1983 had rescued more than 30,000 animals: 36,000, according to Itaipu; almost 39,000, according to BBC News Brasil, adding both sides.

06Who stood in the way

The 1,350 km² lake covered inhabited land. On the Brazilian side, 8,519 properties were affected, 6,913 rural and 1,606 urban, about 101,000 hectares. The number of people varies with the method: about 42,000, 40,000 or 60,000 affected, depending on the source. According to the Paraná State Truth Commission, Itaipu compensated schools, churches, hospitals and businesses first, emptying out the communities. On the Paraguayan side, the total of non-Indigenous people expropriated does not appear in the sources consulted.

For the Avá-Guarani, the count was even shorter. In 1977, a working group from Funai and Incra recognized only 11 Indigenous families, 27 people, a figure the Paraná Truth Commission calls a falsification of reality. The Guarani claimed 1,500 hectares; in 1982 they received a registered area of 231.88 hectares. Photos from 1981, revealed in 2017, show Guarani houses in flames in front of officials.

Redress came four decades later. On 24 March 2025, the STF, Brazil's Supreme Federal Court, approved a settlement providing for the purchase of 3,000 hectares for the Avá-Guarani, with R$240 million from Itaipu. On 29 October 2025, the Brazilian federal government, Funai, Incra and Itaipu published an apology for the human rights violations committed against this people.

07The plant and the bill that remained

The first unit generated power on 5 May 1984, and the official inauguration, with Figueiredo and Stroessner, was on 25 October of that year. The 18th unit came online in 1991; the last two, in 2006 and 2007. In 2016, Itaipu produced 103,098,366 MWh, the annual world record at the time, surpassed by China's Three Gorges in 2020.

The cost of construction also has more than one version: about 12 billion dollars, according to Memória da Eletricidade, or about 14 billion, according to Itaipu itself. The job was almost entirely financed with loans, and the debt was paid off on 28 February 2023, after about 63 billion dollars paid over fifty years, of which 27.9 billion in interest and charges.

Fifty years after the Treaty came into force, in 2023, it called for a revision of the plant's financial terms, the so-called Annex C. In September 2026, Brazil and Paraguay had still not reached an agreement.

MYTHSWhat people say and what the record shows

  • What people say

    149 workers died, the official number.

    What the record shows

    The 149 appears on English-language websites without any source. The number Itaipu itself gave, in 2023, is an estimate of 106 deaths between 1978 and 1984; for the other years of construction there is no official number.

  • What people say

    There are workers buried in the dam's concrete.

    What the record shows

    It is a persistent memory among former workers, recorded by researchers and by the Paraná State Truth Commission as an account. There is no name, case or document.

  • What people say

    Itaipu cost 63 billion dollars.

    What the record shows

    That is the total paid on the debt over fifty years, with 27.9 billion in interest and charges. Construction is estimated at about 12 to 14 billion dollars of the time, depending on the source.

  • What people say

    Itaipu is the largest power plant in the world.

    What the record shows

    In installed capacity, Three Gorges, in China, has 22,500 MW against 14,000; in annual output, it has held the record since 2020. Itaipu held the annual record in 2016.

  • What people say

    Sete Quedas was the largest waterfall in the world by volume.

    What the record shows

    The flow of 13,300 m³/s repeated by Brazilian sources has no primary reference, and by the average cited, other falls, such as Boyoma, in the Congo, exceed it. What holds up: one of the largest in the world by volume of water.

QUESTIONSFrequently asked questions

When was the Itaipu Dam built?

Work began in 1975, the river was diverted on 20 October 1978 and the gates were closed on 13 October 1982. The first unit generated power on 5 May 1984, and the inauguration was on 25 October 1984; the last two units came online in 2006 and 2007.

How was the Itaipu Dam built?

First they took the Paraná River out of its bed: a diversion channel about 2 km long was opened on the bank, and on 20 October 1978, 58 tons of explosives brought down the cofferdams. With the bed dry, the dam was raised, with more than 12 million m³ of concrete.

How many people died building the Itaipu Dam?

There is no official number for the whole job. Itaipu itself estimates 106 deaths between 1978 and 1984; other, partial counts range from 40 to 138, and undocumented accounts speak of up to 800 or about a thousand.

Is Itaipu the largest hydroelectric plant in the world?

No. In installed capacity, Three Gorges, in China, has 22,500 MW against Itaipu's 14,000, and it has held the record for annual output since 2020. Itaipu held that record in 2016.

How much did the Itaipu Dam cost?

The sources disagree: about 12 billion dollars of the time, according to Memória da Eletricidade, or about 14 billion, according to Itaipu. The 63 billion that circulates is the total paid on the debt over fifty years, with 27.9 billion in interest and charges.
